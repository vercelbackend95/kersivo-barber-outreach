# What a No-Show Really Costs a Barbershop — and When a Deposit Makes Sense
*General information for UK barbershop owners, not legal advice. Law, guidance and product details checked 15–16 October 2026. One legal question, the 14-day cancellation right for online bookings, is unsettled and hasn't been reviewed by a lawyer; see "The question the law hasn't settled".*
An empty chair at 3pm on a Tuesday and an empty chair at 10.30am on a Saturday look the same in the diary. They aren't the same loss. One is usually money you'll never see again. The other is often a gap that a walk-in fills before the kettle boils.
That difference is the whole case for, or against, a deposit. Work out what a missed appointment really costs once you count what comes back, and you'll know whether a deposit is worth the friction, how big it can sensibly be, and where the law starts to push back.

## What comes back after a no-show
The headline loss is the price of the cut. The real loss is the price minus three things.
**The chance someone else takes the slot.** A busy Saturday morning has a good chance of a refill; a quiet weekday afternoon probably doesn't. Notice matters too.
**Whether the client rebooks.** A rebooking into a slot that would otherwise be empty delays the cut rather than losing it. In a full diary, the loss stands.
**The costs that vanish with the appointment.** Products you didn't use and the card fee you didn't pay are savings. Wages usually aren't: an employed barber on fixed pay costs the same whether the chair is full or empty, so wages aren't an extra cost of the no-show.

## A worked example
Every figure here is an **illustrative assumption** to show the method, not data about UK barbershops. There is no reliable public data on barbershop no-show rates, so use your own diary.

**Assumptions:**
- a £25 cut, with £1.00 of products per cut;
- a card fee of 1.5% + 20p on each separate card payment (check your own provider's rates);
- **with a deposit:** £5 paid online at booking, then a £20 balance paid by card in the shop. That's two card payments, costing £0.275 + £0.50 = £0.775;
- **without a deposit:** £25 paid by card in the shop, one payment costing £0.575;
- on a no-show, the £5 deposit is kept. Its card fee was already paid at booking and isn't recovered;
- if the slot is refilled, it's by a walk-in paying £25 by card in one payment (£0.575 fee), and the deposit is still kept;
- no rebooking; fixed wages excluded, because they're paid either way.

These are losses of **contribution**, and they depend on the assumptions above, especially that a walk-in refill pays £25 in one card payment. Because rent, rates and fixed wages don't change when one client fails to turn up, the shop's profit for the period falls by the same amount. That isn't the shop's final profit, which depends on all its fixed costs.
Three details are easy to miss:
- **Split payments.** A deposit plus a balance means two fixed card charges: £0.775 instead of £0.575, on every deposit booking that turns up.
- **How the refill pays.** If the person who takes the empty slot also books online, with their own deposit and balance, the refill earns £0.20 less and the expected losses rise slightly, to about £4.57 on Saturday and £16.18 on Tuesday.
- **Refunding when refilled.** The deposit is assumed to be kept even when the slot is refilled. If your policy refunds it in that case, the Saturday figure rises. Keeping a deposit when you lost nothing is also harder to justify under the CMA's "net loss" test discussed below.
The busy Saturday no-show is the cheap one. The quiet weekday is where the money goes, and where a deposit does most of its work.

## Who carries the loss
How the chair is run decides who loses.
- **Owner-operator or employed team:** the shop carries the loss above.
- **Self-employed renter on fixed chair rent:** the renter loses the cut; the shop's rent arrives either way.
- **Commission or percentage arrangement:** the loss is shared roughly in line with the split.
Who is responsible for the deposit terms follows the contract, not just who receives the payment. Put it in writing.

## Is £5 a "small" deposit?
UK consumer law doesn't stop you taking a deposit. It limits when you can keep one.
Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, an unfair term isn't binding on the client (section 62), and written terms must be plain and intelligible (section 68). Terms that may be unfair include requiring a client who cancels or doesn't turn up to pay a "disproportionately high sum" (Schedule 2, paragraphs 4 to 6).
The Competition and Markets Authority's guidance (CMA37, updated 22 July 2026) says keeping a prepayment is more likely to be fair where it reflects your "unavoidable net costs or the net loss of profit" (para 6.62). It treats a genuine deposit, made clear at the earliest opportunity and kept only in "clear and narrow" circumstances, as acceptable, and adds that such a deposit "will not normally be more than a small percentage of the price".
So be honest about the numbers. £5 is 20% of a £25 cut, 42% of a £12 kids' cut and about 8% of a £60 service. Neither the law nor the CMA gives a safe percentage. What helps is keeping the deposit below the loss you can show, as in the Tuesday example, though that doesn't make it fair automatically.
Circumstances matter as much as the amount. A no-show is the strongest case for keeping a deposit; a cancellation with plenty of notice is much weaker. If *you* cancel, refund in full. The CMA also warns against being compensated twice for the same loss (CMA37, footnote 258), so don't keep a deposit and charge a separate no-show fee for the same appointment.

## The question the law hasn't settled
**Warning: this point is unresolved. Don't build a strict non-refundable policy for online bookings on an assumption about it.**
Bookings made online or by other distance means can carry a 14-day right to cancel under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013. Bookings made in person at the shop don't. The regulations exempt "services related to leisure activities" booked for a specific date. Whether a haircut is a leisure activity hasn't been decided by any UK court or regulator, and there are reasonable arguments on both sides. Phone requests for an appointment may not count as distance bookings at all.
If the exemption doesn't apply, a client who books online could cancel within 14 days of booking. A deposit for an appointment inside that window could then be refundable, and you'd also need to tell clients before they book whether they have a right to cancel. If you don't, the cancellation period can stretch to up to 12 months.
This guide doesn't answer the question, and no legal review of it has taken place. If most of your deposits come from online bookings made more than a day or two ahead, get advice from a consumer-law specialist before relying on a strict no-refund rule.
Price display is a separate point. A deposit that counts towards the price isn't an extra fee. A compulsory booking fee on top of the price is, and under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 the total price you show should include fees the client "will necessarily incur" (section 230).

## The case against a deposit
A deposit adds a step to booking, and some clients will book elsewhere or walk in instead. Loyal regulars who have never missed an appointment may resent it. We found no reliable UK evidence on how much deposits cut barbershop no-shows.
If no-shows cluster in a few slots or among a few clients, a targeted rule, such as deposits for new clients or weekday afternoons, may protect more money with less friction. If no-shows are rare and usually refilled, you may not need a deposit at all.

## Before you set a policy
- **Measure.** From two or three months of diary, count no-shows by day and time, refills and rebookings.
- **Separate the cases.** No-show, late cancellation, early cancellation and the shop cancelling each deserve a different answer.
- **Show it before booking,** in plain words, on the booking page and the confirmation.
- **Refund in full when you cancel,** and promptly.
- **Never charge twice** for the same missed appointment.
- **Check the 14-day question** for online bookings, and take specialist advice if deposits matter to your income.
- **Agree in writing** with renters whose policy applies and who holds the money.

## The decision
Start with your diary, not a template. If your real losses are small and fall on busy days, reminders and a waiting list may be enough. If they're large and concentrated in quiet slots, a deposit in proportion to the price can be justified for those bookings. Use it only where a no-show actually causes a loss, make it clear before anyone books, refund it when the fault is yours, and get the 14-day question checked if most of your bookings are made online.
